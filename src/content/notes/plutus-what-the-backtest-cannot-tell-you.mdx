## The first bot lost to SPY, so I kept the one signal that worked

The first bot had five signals: price trend, a fundamentals score, a "moat" score, news sentiment and a layer that asked a language model for a view. Then I attributed its returns signal by signal with a point-in-time backtest.

One signal had predictive power. Price trend, with an information coefficient around 0.15. The rest measured zero or negative, and they were weighting the portfolio anyway. The point-in-time backtest of the whole thing lost to SPY, with an 82% drawdown along the way.

Its backtester was also lying to me. It marked a delisted stock at what I had paid for it, so a stock that fell 95% snapped back to full cost. A market-cap gate let through any stock whose size it could not find. A fabricated signal fed the years 2018 to 2022. One test now corrupts every price after a cut date and fails if anything before the cut changes.

## The strategy is one signal and two overlays

Rank by 6- and 12-month momentum, skipping the most recent 10 trading days. Hold only names above their own 200-day average. If SPY is below its own 200-day average, the book goes risk-off into bonds, gold or bills by their own 6-month momentum. Size the whole thing to a 30% ex-ante volatility. Rebalance monthly.

## The headline is 26.1% a year, and it is the flattering number

From January 2007 to October 2026, 1,107 tickers, $100k start, 10 bps per side, with the two 10% sleeves described further down included:

2022 was −23%, worse than SPY's −18%: a trend filter is late by construction, and that year the usual risk-off assets fell with stocks.

## What the number cannot say

**Survivorship bias is only partly corrected.** The universe is today's S&P 500 and S&P 400 members plus names that were removed but are still listed, which softens the bias without removing it. An equal-weight buy-and-hold of the backtest universe beats SPY by 6.3% a year, a rough size for what remains. Any strategy on this universe starts with a tailwind that will not exist going forward. The first figure is the test: on the members of each month the same code earned 3.1% a year, and my own README says to expect the live stock book to track SPY within a few points.

**It is one path.** A stationary block bootstrap of daily returns puts the Sharpe's 95% interval at [0.62, 1.47] and the CAGR's at [+13.5%, +39.8%]. The median bootstrapped drawdown is −37%; the fifth percentile is −52%.

The strategy family, momentum plus trend filter plus vol targeting, was chosen from the literature rather than fitted here. The defaults were still picked knowing this history. That is selection, and it does not wash out.

**Turnover is high.** 419% a year one-way, 4,347 trades. The 10 bps assumption is defensible for large caps at the open; it is not free.

## Every idea I rejected is still a config option

Each is defaulted off, with its result recorded next to it, so the next me does not re-run the experiment.

| Idea | Source | Result here |
|---|---|---|
| 20% hard stop-loss (also 15%, 30%) | Han, Zhou & Zhu 2016 | Sharpe −0.01 to −0.03, worst year slightly worse: whipsaw |
| Daily regime exit, sell when SPY crosses its average mid-month | common practice | CAGR −1.3%, worst year −18% against −13%: whipsaw |
| Ensemble of 100- and 200-day filters | Faber variants | Sharpe −0.04 |
| Leverage at 1.5× and 2× | | Lower CAGR, deeper drawdowns: borrow cost eats the extra return |
| Add S&P 600 small caps (1,708 names) | | Sharpe 0.58 to 0.73, drawdowns −44% to −55%: worse per unit of risk for barely more return |

Two sleeves held up in this same backtest and run in the paper account at 10% each: a leveraged Nasdaq ETF held only while the market filter is on, and a bitcoin slice gated on its own 200-day average. These are the knobs I would turn off first if I stopped believing the backtest.

## The paper account is the real test

The strategy runs on a timer in my homelab against a paper account, not real money. The repository is private; it has keys in its deployment path and the state of a brokerage account. Deploying never places an order. The trader runs once per weekday morning and writes a JSON record of what it decided and why; that record is what I read, not the broker's dashboard.

The code was written by coding agents under my direction; the experiment design, the bar for evidence and the verdicts are mine.
